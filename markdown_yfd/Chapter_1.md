## Chapter 1. Dark Alchemy

The Apothecary of Mantua descended the narrow stairway into the Capulet family burial vault, his rusted lantern swinging with each careful step. Its feeble light danced across the carved stone walls, casting his shadow as a gaunt herald of resurrection against elaborate reliefs depicting the Judgment Day. The air hung heavy with the mingled scents of incense, candle wax, and the sweet undertone of decay, while the morning dew rendered each stone treacherous beneath his worn leather boots. He clutched a wicker basket against his chest, its contents clinking softly; iron collars, sturdy shackles, and several vials of turbid resurrection serum nestled in straw.

The sepulcher opened before him as he reached the bottom stair, revealing a chamber far grander than its humble entrance suggested. Vaulted ceilings arched overhead, supported by columns carved with the Capulet family crest: a falcon with outstretched wings clutching a rose in its talons. Marble statues of stern-faced patriarchs kept their eternal vigil from niches in the walls, their cold eyes seeming to follow his movements with aristocratic disapproval. Bronze sconces, now cold and empty, lined the walls where mourners' candles had burned during yesterday's hasty interment ceremony. Their wax drippings formed stalactites along the ornate stonework, evidence of centuries of Capulet grief.

The Apothecary inhaled deeply, tasting metal on his tongue, the particular flavor of wealth intermingled with mortality. His fingers, sensitized by years of measuring precise quantities of herbs and minerals, detected subtle temperature variations as he moved deeper into the crypt; the ancient stones radiated a chill that penetrated his thin garments, while the newer section, recently opened for the tragic lovers, retained some warmth from yesterday's funeral proceedings. The residue of expensive incense clung to the elaborate carvings, masking but not eliminating the underlying scent of human remains in various states of dissolution.

He paused before the marble slab where Verona's most tragic lovers had been laid to rest, raising his lantern to better examine their coffins. Unlike the weathered stone sarcophagi of their ancestors, Romeo and Juliet reposed in wooden caskets, temporary accommodations until proper monuments could be commissioned. The Montague boy's coffin appeared plain, hastily constructed, while the Capulet girl's featured delicate carvings of lilies and angels, befitting her station. The Apothecary set his basket upon the cold floor with practiced care, a thin smile forming on his lips.

"Not poison I gave you, young Romeo," he murmured, his voice scarcely disturbing the sacred silence. "Something far more valuable waits in your veins, something far more profitable for us both." He extracted a vial from his basket and held it to the lantern light, admiring the cloudy liquid that would soon complete what he had begun.

The serum's murky consistency reminded him of his first successful experiment three winters past: a small gray cat found frozen in the snow outside his laboratory. The creature had been perfectly dead, stiff with cold and without heartbeat for hours when he injected the prototype formula directly into its heart. He recalled with perfect clarity how the animal had convulsed, then gone still, only to open its eyes twelve hours later with an unnatural yellow glow. The cat had lived another fortnight in his care, following his movements with unsettling attention, obeying simple commands with uncharacteristic docility. When it finally collapsed, its internal organs had crystallized into something neither fully organic nor mineral, a transformation that had informed every subsequent refinement of his formula.

According to his calculations, the first stage of the serum had induced a deathlike slumber indistinguishable from true mortality; the second stage, however, would bring reanimation with certain useful alterations to the temperament and faculties. The Apothecary had administered hundreds of poisons throughout his career but had reserved this particular concoction for an opportunity worthy of its potential. Romeo's desperate plea had presented just such a moment, transforming a mere transaction into the culmination of decades of forbidden research.

Love and loyalty were merely chemical processes after all, he had long reasoned, and therefore subject to manipulation by one with sufficient knowledge and daring. The heart that beat for a lover could be redirected to beat for a master; the mind that worshipped beauty could be recalibrated to revere authority. Science, not sorcery, would transform these privileged children into the first of his devoted servants.

A faint scraping sound interrupted his contemplation. The Apothecary froze, his breath catching in his throat as he slowly turned toward Juliet's coffin. The noise came again, nails scratching against wood in a deliberate, rhythmic pattern like a prisoner tapping messages through a dungeon wall. The girl awakened first, despite receiving the serum second, an interesting deviation from his previous trials with animals and condemned criminals.

His heart accelerated as he approached her coffin, listening to the persistent scratching that betrayed her resurrection. The sound transported him instantly to his laboratory five years prior, when his first successful reanimation, a hanged thief from Milan, had similarly announced his return with desperate scratching before breaking free and requiring immediate termination. The memory caused sweat to bead along the Apothecary's hairline despite the crypt's chill.

The scratching intensified, becoming more purposeful and direct. He placed his hand upon the coffin lid, feeling the vibrations of Juliet's efforts through the wood. Early light began to seep through a narrow window slit high in the eastern wall, painting a thin golden line across the stone floor. Outside, the distant bells of Verona's cathedral chimed the quarter-hour before dawn, reminding him that time pressed ever forward. Soon the churchyard above would stir with morning prayers and grave-tenders.

"Patience, sweet lady," the Apothecary crooned, patting the coffin lid with mock tenderness. "Your new life awaits, and with it, purpose beyond the follies of childish passion." He leaned closer, his lips nearly touching the wood. "You shall serve me well, and in serving, find greater meaning than love ever offered."

From within the coffin came a muffled response, so faint he might have imagined it: "My grave... like my wedding bed."

The Apothecary straightened, satisfaction warming his chest. Memory fragments were common in the early stages of reanimation, the subject's final thoughts often lingered, providing convenient anchors for establishing control. He had expected Romeo's despair might make him more malleable, but perhaps Juliet's romantic fatalism would prove equally useful.

"Yes, child," he replied, his tone deliberately soothing. "From death to new purpose, as from maiden to wife. A transformation most profound awaits you."

The scratching ceased abruptly, replaced by silence so complete the Apothecary found himself holding his breath to better listen. After several heartbeats, a single word emerged from within the coffin, spoken with unnerving clarity: "Romeo?"

The Apothecary hesitated, caught in a moment of scientific indecision. Should he secure the lovers before their full awakening or allow the serum's second stage to unfold without interference? Observation suggested the latter might yield valuable insights, while prudence advocated for the former. He glanced at the restraints in his basket, calculating the risk of each approach with the cool detachment of a natural philosopher.

His past experiments offered contradictory guidance. The miller's son from Padua had awakened docile and confused, easily directed into servitude until his brother discovered and destroyed him. The twins from Venice, however, had emerged with such violence that three assistants perished before the subjects could be properly restrained. The composition of this particular batch most closely resembled the formula used on the Venetian pair, but he had modified the binding agent and reduced the stimulant properties specifically to avoid such uncontrolled awakening.

The scratching from Juliet's coffin diminished momentarily, then resumed with altered rhythm, slower, more deliberate. Perhaps she conserved her strength or adapted to her confinement. The Apothecary circled both coffins, noting the complete silence from Romeo's casket with growing concern. Different reactions to identical serums suggested variables he had not accounted for: perhaps the lovers' youth, their extreme emotional states at death, or some quality in the Veronese water that had constituted their final drinks.

"Romeo is waiting," he lied smoothly, returning to Juliet's coffin. "He serves me now, and soon you shall join him. Together in life as you wished to be in death."

"Banish'd," came the hollow response, the word elongated and distorted as if spoken underwater.

The Apothecary frowned. "No longer separated, but united in purpose," he countered, his tone hardening slightly. "You will obey, as he obeys."

His deliberation ended abruptly when a thunderous crack split the sepulchral quiet. The Apothecary whirled toward Romeo's coffin, where wood splintered outward near the center. A second impact followed, producing a jagged fissure along the lid's length. The third blow punched through completely as an unnaturally strong hand erupted through the wood, pale fingers flexing in the crypt's cool air.

The hand, Romeo's hand, appeared bloodless yet supple, neither the stiff appendage of rigor mortis nor the withered claw of desiccation. The nails retained their healthy pink hue, and the skin showed none of the mottling typically associated with death. Those fingers stretched wide, testing their range of motion before clenching into a fist and smashing downward against the fractured wood.

Fragments of oak scattered across the marble as the hand gripped the fractured lid and tore it aside with strength no living man could muster. The wood separated along its grain with a sound like distant thunder, echoing through the burial chamber and sending dust motes swirling in the lantern light. The calculation had been imprecise. The Apothecary lunged for his basket, seizing the nearest leather collar while muttering a string of alchemical corrections under his breath.

"Be still, boy," he commanded sharply. "Your mistress awakens, and you shall both—"

"Juliet?" Romeo interrupted, the name emerging as a rasp from unused vocal cords.

The Apothecary's words died in his throat as Romeo rose from his coffin with unnatural grace, his movements fluid yet somehow wrong, as though his limbs obeyed different laws of motion than those governing ordinary men. His burial clothes, a simple white shroud provided by the Friar, hung in tatters around his shoulders, torn during his violent emergence. His skin bore a waxy pallor that caught the lantern light strangely, neither reflecting nor absorbing it properly. His eyes, once warm with passionate youth, now reflected the flame with cold intensity.

"Be still and accept your collar," the Apothecary insisted, advancing with the restraint extended. "Your strength is impressive but temporary. Soon you will weaken without my treatments."

Romeo's gaze shifted from the Apothecary to the collar, then back again. His lips parted slightly, revealing teeth that seemed somehow sharper than before. "Dagger," he murmured, one hand rising to touch his chest where the fatal wound had been. "Poison. You."

"Not poison but rebirth," the Apothecary corrected, positioning himself between the two coffins to maintain sight of both subjects. "I have given you life beyond death, and in return, you owe me service."

With unexpected swiftness, Romeo's hand shot out, seizing the collar and crushing it in his grip. The leather cracked and the metal buckle bent under pressure that would have shattered living bones. The Apothecary stumbled backward, his lantern swinging wildly and nearly extinguishing as oil sloshed inside its reservoir. Shadows danced across the crypt in nauseating patterns, momentarily plunging Romeo's face into darkness before revealing it again with unsettling new aspects: deeper hollows beneath the cheekbones, a bluish tinge to the lips, pupils expanded to consume the iris entirely.

The Apothecary retreated further, fumbling in his pocket for the sedative tincture he had prepared as a contingency. His fingers closed around the small vial just as Romeo advanced, moving with a predatory grace that covered the distance between them in half the time a living man would require. The lantern light stabilized, revealing Romeo's expression, not anger but something more primal, an intensity of focus that reminded the Apothecary of a wolf tracking wounded prey.

"You will obey me," the Apothecary insisted, his voice rising in pitch despite his efforts to maintain authority. "The formula binds you to my will, as it has bound others before you."

Romeo paused, head tilting slightly as if listening to distant music. "Others?" he repeated, the word emerging with unexpected clarity.

Behind the Apothecary, wood cracked and splintered as Juliet's coffin surrendered to her resurrection. He dared not turn, keeping his attention fixed on the more immediate threat before him. Romeo's gaze shifted to a point over the Apothecary's shoulder, his expression softening into something approaching recognition.

The Apothecary seized this moment of distraction to uncork the sedative vial with his thumb, preparing to dash the contents into Romeo's face. Before he could complete the motion, Romeo's hand closed around his wrist with crushing force. The vial slipped from suddenly nerveless fingers, shattering against the stone floor and releasing a sharp herbal scent that dissipated uselessly into the crypt's stale air.

"Release me," the Apothecary demanded, struggling against a grip that felt like iron bands around his flesh. "I am your master, your creator!"

Romeo leaned closer, his face inches from the Apothecary's own. No breath emerged from those slightly parted lips as he whispered: "Death... is my master now."

The Apothecary's mind raced through revised strategies even as his arm began to numb from Romeo's grip. The iron manacles in his basket suddenly seemed inadequate against the strength displayed in Romeo's awakening. Perhaps chemical restraint would prove more effective than physical bindings; the stronger paralytic compound in his inner pocket might subdue them until he could transport them to his laboratory. Or perhaps appeal to their lingering humanity might succeed where force could not.

"You loved her in life," the Apothecary gasped, nodding toward the sound of Juliet's emergence. "In this new existence, you may love her eternally, without families to separate you, without death to part you. I offer this gift freely."

Romeo's grip loosened slightly, uncertainty flickering across his features. The Apothecary pressed his advantage, his voice softening into persuasive tones that had convinced princes and peasants alike to purchase his most expensive remedies.

"Think of it, Romeo Montague. No more Capulets and Montagues, no more feuds or banishments. Only you and your Juliet, together through the ages, bound by something stronger than mere mortality."

When the Apothecary finally risked a glance over his shoulder, he found Juliet standing beside her shattered coffin, clad in her burial gown of silver brocade now stained with the dust of resurrection. Her golden hair, arranged by mourners in an elaborate style befitting her final appearance, remained perfectly coiffed despite her violent emergence. The cosmetics applied to mask death's pallor had smeared slightly, giving her the appearance of a porcelain doll partially melted near a flame.

"Juliet," Romeo called, his voice stronger now, more certain.

"Romeo," she answered, the name emerging with perfect clarity.

The realization struck the Apothecary with chilling clarity: his formulation had produced effects far beyond his expectations. The physical power displayed in their awakening exceeded all previous experiments; the restraints he had brought would prove woefully inadequate. More concerning still was the silent communication passing between the subjects. Romeo's gaze had shifted from the Apothecary to Juliet, and something unspoken transferred between them, some recognition that excluded their creator entirely.

Romeo released the Apothecary's wrist, leaving behind a perfect ring of bruises already darkening to purple. The Apothecary rubbed his injured arm, calculating his diminishing options as Juliet stepped forward, her movements displaying the same uncanny fluidity as Romeo's. Her pale feet made no sound against the stone floor, creating an impression of gliding rather than walking.

"You are bound to me," the Apothecary insisted, reaching for his final vial of control serum. "The formula in your veins requires regular supplementation, which only I can provide. Without it, you will deteriorate into mindless husks within days."

Neither responded to this threat, yet they moved in perfect coordination, as if guided by a single mind housed in separate bodies. Their expressions remained blank yet somehow ravenous, a hunger not of the flesh but of some deeper, more fundamental nature. When Romeo spoke again, his voice overlapped with Juliet's, creating an unsettling harmony:

"What is done cannot be undone."

The Apothecary retreated one step, then another, his scientific fascination warring with newfound fear. The lovers advanced in perfect synchrony, their eyes fixed upon him with an unsettling, unified purpose. He had succeeded in cheating death and binding these star-crossed lovers to the mortal plane, but as they approached with inexorable determination, he understood with terrible clarity that he had not succeeded in binding them to him.

"Stay back," he commanded, his voice cracking as he fumbled with the stopper of his final vial. "I created you; I can unmake you just as easily!"

Behind the advancing pair, a shadow shifted near the far wall of the crypt, where older Capulet remains rested in stone sarcophagi. The Apothecary narrowed his eyes, straining to determine if the movement was merely a trick of his swinging lantern or something more substantial. A soft scraping sound, distinct from any the lovers had produced, emanated from the darkened corner where the elaborate tomb of Juliet's grandfather dominated the chamber.

The lovers paused their advance, heads turning in unison toward the sound. They move together.

From the shadows beyond the lantern's reach came a whispered voice, speaking words in no language the Apothecary recognized, guttural syllables that seemed to resonate with the stone itself. The temperature in the crypt plummeted suddenly, causing the lantern flame to sputter and dim. Romeo and Juliet remained motionless, listening to the whispering with evident recognition, while frost began to form intricate patterns along the edges of the marble slab that had supported their coffins.

The sealed door of Old Capulet's tomb creaked inward, releasing the scent of much older death into the chamber. The Apothecary's final vial slipped from trembling fingers as he realized with dawning horror that his resurrection serum might have awakened far more than the two young lovers of Verona.
