# Chapter 12: The Bleeding City

Shouts of panic echoed through the shrouded streets of Verona, where dawn broke crimson against the horizon. The Apothecary of Mantua emerged from the catacombs into a city transformed by terror. His legs trembled with exhaustion, his lungs burning from the ascent, but it was the sight before him that truly stole his breath.

Verona bled.

Not with the metaphorical wounds of conflict, but with literal rivers of crimson that traced the gutters and pooled at intersections. Bodies lay where they had fallen—some bearing the telltale puncture wounds of Romeo and Juliet's feeding, others trampled in the panicked flight of their neighbors. The corruption beneath the Apothecary's robes pulsed in sympathetic rhythm with this urban decay, each throb a reminder of his unwitting complicity.

"I did this," he whispered, the admission torn from him by the weight of evidence. His scientific detachment crumbled before the human cost of his ambition—the formula that had been his life's achievement now revealed as catastrophe incarnate.

A woman ran past, clutching a child to her breast, her eyes wild with terror. Behind her came others, a stream of humanity flowing away from the city center like blood from an opened vein. The Apothecary caught fragments of their panicked cries:

"They rise from their graves!"
"The dead walk among us!"
"God has abandoned Verona!"

Church bells tolled incessantly, their bronze voices competing with human screams in a cacophony of desperation. The Apothecary moved against this tide of fleeing citizens, each step requiring deliberate force of will. The spreading corruption across his chest seemed to gain weight with his guilt, dragging at his limbs as he navigated the chaos.

Near the central piazza, a massive statue of St. Peter lay toppled and broken, its stone head separated from the body and staring skyward with blank, accusatory eyes. The sight struck the Apothecary with symbolic force—faith itself had fallen in Verona, leaving only science and superstition to battle the encroaching darkness.

"The binding weakens faster than anticipated," he muttered, his scientific mind automatically analyzing despite his horror. "The entity's influence spreads exponentially with each new vessel created."

A city guard stumbled past, his crimson sash torn and bloodied, his sword arm hanging useless at his side. "Captain Bellini holds the cathedral square," he gasped when the Apothecary caught his shoulder. "Go there if you seek shelter. Nowhere else remains safe."

Before the Apothecary could question him further, the guard's eyes widened at something beyond his shoulder. Without another word, the man tore free and fled, leaving the Apothecary to turn slowly toward whatever had inspired such terror.

A procession moved through the street behind him—not the orderly religious pageantry Verona was known for, but a shambling parade of the recently deceased. Their movements carried that fluid grace he had observed in Romeo and Juliet, though less refined, more mechanical. Their eyes reflected the morning light with cold intensity, and their skin bore the waxy pallor of interrupted decay.

At their head walked a figure the Apothecary recognized with a jolt of horror—the elderly groundskeeper from the cemetery, his weathered face now smooth with the tranquility of the transformed. In his gnarled hands, he carried a heart that pulsed with blue luminescence, identical to the one Romeo had displayed in the catacombs.

"Another vessel," the Apothecary whispered, understanding dawning with terrible clarity. "They harvest the hearts to strengthen the entity's connection to our world."

Muffled cries guided him toward a shadowed alley, the source of despair an all too familiar tableau. A small group of the newly risen had cornered a merchant and his young son against a stone wall. The man stood before his child, a broken chair leg clutched in white-knuckled hands, his face a mask of desperate courage.

The Apothecary hesitated only briefly before retrieving a vial from within his robes—one of several compounds he had prepared after studying the ancient texts with Friar Lawrence. The formula contained elements of his resurrection serum, modified with components designed to disrupt the entity's connection to its vessels.

"A theory untested," he admitted to himself, uncorking the vial with trembling fingers. "But necessity demands action before certainty."

With practiced precision, he flung the contents toward the undead assailants. The liquid caught the morning light, separating into glistening droplets that spattered across pale, waxy skin. For a moment, nothing happened—then the nearest vessel convulsed, its movements losing that unnatural fluidity as the chemical compound disrupted the entity's control.

The Apothecary seized this moment of confusion to rush forward, grasping the merchant's arm. "Run!" he commanded, pulling the man and child toward the alley's opposite end. "They will recover quickly!"

As they fled, the Apothecary glanced back and froze in mid-stride. One of the vessels had turned toward him, its face now fully visible in the strengthening daylight. Recognition struck like a physical blow—Silvio Contarini, the Venetian emissary, his elegant features now transformed by the entity's influence.

Their eyes met across the narrowing distance, and something like recognition flickered in Contarini's gaze—not the mindless hunger of the other vessels, but a more focused malevolence. The corruption beneath the Apothecary's robes pulsed painfully, responding to this silent acknowledgment between marked and transformed.

"The Republic's reach extends even here," the Apothecary murmured, backing away as Contarini took a single, deliberate step forward. "Was this your mission all along, Emissary? To assess the entity's potential as a weapon for Venice?"

No answer came from those cold lips, but the question lingered as the Apothecary turned and fled, guiding the merchant and his son through Verona's labyrinthine streets. They emerged near a small chapel where a crowd had gathered, drawn by the flicker of candlelight and the promise of sanctuary.

Around the next corner, a flicker of candlelight offered fleeting warmth amidst a crowd of the living, fearful and cold. Friar Lawrence stood upon the chapel steps, his grey robes stained with blood not his own, his weathered face grave as he dispensed comfort and direction to the frightened citizens. A makeshift barricade of overturned carts and broken furniture protected the chapel entrance, manned by grim-faced men with improvised weapons.

"Apothecary," the Friar called, relief evident in his voice as he descended the steps. "I feared you lost to the catacombs—or worse, to the corruption."

"The corruption advances," the Apothecary admitted, parting his robes to reveal the blue-black tendrils that now covered most of his chest in patterns matching the binding diagrams. "But my mind remains my own, for now."

They withdrew to the chapel's side altar, where Friar Lawrence had established a crude laboratory using components salvaged from the Apothecary's shop. Herbs hung in drying bunches from the rafters, their pungent aromas mingling with incense and fear-sweat. Glass vials and ceramic mortars covered every surface, evidence of their desperate experimentation.

The Friar's crucifix dangled as he leaned forward, casting a long shadow against the torch-lit stone wall. "Captain Bellini holds the cathedral with what remains of the city guard," he reported, his voice low to avoid spreading panic. "But their numbers dwindle with each hour. The vessels target those with authority first—guards, magistrates, clergy."

"A strategy that suggests intelligence beyond mere hunger," the Apothecary observed, his scientific mind cataloging this new information. "The entity directs its vessels with purpose, dismantling the city's defenses systematically."

"What of Romeo and Juliet?" Friar Lawrence asked, his gaze dropping to the corruption spreading across the Apothecary's chest. "Have they progressed as you have?"

"Far beyond," the Apothecary replied, recalling their transformed appearance in the catacombs. "They have become primary conduits for the entity's influence, harvesting hearts to strengthen its connection to our world. And they marked me as the thirteenth vessel—the final heart needed to complete the awakening."

The Friar's expression darkened. "Then our time grows shorter than I feared. The modified formula—"

"Shows promise but lacks potency," the Apothecary finished, gesturing toward the vials arranged on the altar. "I disrupted a vessel's connection temporarily, but the effect proved fleeting. We require something stronger to sever Romeo and Juliet from the entity's control."

Their discussion was interrupted by a commotion at the barricade. A woman had arrived, her clothes torn and bloodied, her eyes wild with terror. "The cathedral is breached!" she cried, collapsing into the arms of those who rushed to assist her. "Captain Bellini falls back to the inner sanctuary, but the dead come in waves!"

Panic rippled through the gathered survivors, threatening to dissolve what little order remained. The Apothecary watched as Friar Lawrence moved among them, his calm voice a counterpoint to their fear, his presence a reminder of structure in a world rapidly descending into chaos.

"We cannot remain here," the Friar concluded, returning to the Apothecary's side. "This chapel offers too little protection when night falls again."

"And where would you have us go?" the Apothecary questioned, gesturing toward the barricaded door. "Verona itself has become a hunting ground for the entity's vessels."

Friar Lawrence's gaze turned thoughtful. "Not all of Verona. The monastery on the western hill stands upon consecrated ground, reinforced by wards similar to those in the ancient texts. Its walls are thick, its cellars deep—and crucially, it houses an extensive collection of religious relics that might strengthen our modified formula."

The Apothecary considered this proposal, his scientific mind weighing variables and probabilities. "A defensible position with access to potential components... yes, it offers advantages over this exposed location. But moving these people through Verona's streets in daylight presents considerable risk."

"Less risk than remaining here when darkness falls," the Friar countered. "The vessels grow stronger with each passing hour, their numbers increasing as more citizens are transformed. By nightfall, no barricade will hold them at bay."

Even as the sun set, painting the sky in bruised hues of purple and gold, shadows moved with greater intent. The Apothecary watched from the chapel doorway as Friar Lawrence organized the survivors into groups, assigning stronger members to protect the elderly and children. Their plan, hastily formulated but born of necessity, would see them move through back streets and gardens toward the western hill, avoiding main thoroughfares where the vessels congregated.

Captain Bellini arrived as they completed preparations, his armor dented and bloodied, his remaining men forming a protective ring around the wounded they transported. His weathered face betrayed exhaustion beyond physical limits, yet his eyes remained alert, assessing their makeshift army with professional efficiency.

"The cathedral has fallen," he reported without preamble, his voice hoarse from shouting commands. "These are all that remain of Verona's guard. The vessels target those in authority—they took the Archbishop himself from the altar steps."

"They harvest hearts," the Apothecary explained, gesturing toward his own corrupted chest. "Each one strengthens the entity's connection to our world."

Bellini's gaze sharpened as he noted the spreading discoloration visible at the Apothecary's neckline. "You are infected with whatever plague transforms them?"

"Not plague but something far older," the Apothecary corrected. "An entity that has slumbered beneath Verona for centuries, bound by ancient wards that my formula inadvertently weakened."

The captain's hand moved to his sword hilt, suspicion warring with pragmatism in his expression. "You admit responsibility for this catastrophe?"

"I admit to meddling with forces I did not fully comprehend," the Apothecary acknowledged, meeting Bellini's gaze without flinching. "And I am committed to containing what I have unleashed, if such containment remains possible."

Their tense exchange was interrupted by a child's cry from the chapel doorway. All eyes turned to see a young girl pointing toward the eastern sky, where an unnatural darkness gathered despite the setting sun's persistent glow. The blackness spread like ink in water, tendrils reaching across the heavens to extinguish stars before their proper time.

"It begins," Friar Lawrence whispered, making the sign of the cross. "The entity extends its influence beyond vessels to the natural world itself."

The Apothecary felt the corruption pulse beneath his robes, responding to this manifestation with eager heat. For a terrible moment, something within him yearned toward that spreading darkness, a fragment of consciousness that welcomed the entity's growing power. He clutched his chest, forcing this alien desire down through sheer determination.

"We must move now," he gasped, the effort of resistance leaving him trembling. "Before full night falls and the vessels reach their peak strength."

Bellini nodded curtly, practical concerns overriding his suspicion of the Apothecary. "My men will form the vanguard and rearguard. Distribute your disruptive compounds to those capable of using them effectively."

As they prepared to depart, the Apothecary surveyed the chapel interior one final time. Shattered glass from the rose window crunched beneath his boots, remnants of a desperate struggle earlier in the day. The colored fragments caught the torchlight, casting prismatic reflections across the stone floor—beauty amidst destruction, a reminder of what they fought to preserve.

His gaze fell upon a small vial nestled among the components on the side altar, its contents gleaming with peculiar luminescence. This experimental formula, derived from his resurrection serum but modified according to the ancient texts, represented their fragile hope against the entity's awakening. If properly refined with components from the monastery's reliquary, it might sever Romeo and Juliet's connection to the ancient presence.

Or it might accelerate his own transformation into the thirteenth vessel.

The Apothecary secured the vial within his robes, feeling its weight against his corrupted chest. Science had unleashed this catastrophe; perhaps science might yet contain it. But as he joined the procession of survivors moving cautiously into Verona's darkening streets, the corruption pulsed with another's heartbeat, a constant reminder that time slipped away with each passing moment.

Behind them, the unnatural darkness continued its spread across the sky, swallowing Verona in premature night. And somewhere beneath the city streets, Romeo and Juliet prepared for the final stage of awakening, awaiting only the thirteenth heart to complete their unholy communion.
